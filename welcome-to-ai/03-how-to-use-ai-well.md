# How to Use AI Well

The difference between someone who's "good with AI" and everyone else isn't secret tricks. It's a handful of habits. Here they are.

## 1. Write better prompts (the C.R.A.F.T. checklist)

A weak prompt gets a weak answer. Strong prompts usually have five things:

- **C — Context:** Who you are and what's going on. *"I'm a first-year library science student…"*
- **R — Role:** Who you want the AI to be. *"Act as a patient research librarian."*
- **A — Ask:** The actual task, specific. *"Help me narrow this broad topic into three researchable questions."*
- **F — Format:** How you want the answer. *"Give me a bulleted list, then one paragraph explaining your reasoning."*
- **T — Tone/Tightness:** Length and style. *"Keep it under 200 words, plain English."*

**Weak:** "Tell me about AI in libraries."
**Strong:** "Act as a research librarian. I'm a first-year student writing a 5-page paper. Give me three specific, debatable research questions about AI in public libraries, each with one reason it matters. Bullet points, plain English."

You'll feel the difference immediately.

## 2. Iterate — don't accept the first answer

The first reply is a starting point, not a verdict. Push on it:

- "Make it simpler."
- "What's the strongest counterargument?"
- "You assumed X — redo it assuming Y."
- "Give me three more options, weirder this time."

Conversation beats command. The good stuff usually shows up in round two or three.

## 3. Verify everything factual (the 30-second rule)

> If a fact, name, date, statistic, or citation matters, spend 30 seconds confirming it from a real source before you use it.

Especially **citations**. AI is notorious for inventing real-looking sources — plausible author, plausible title, plausible journal, completely fake. In your field this is a cardinal sin, and your professors *will* catch it. Always check that a source actually exists and actually says what the AI claims. (Library databases — JSTOR, your catalog, Google Scholar — are your truth layer.)

## 4. Never paste in things you shouldn't

Treat a chatbot like a postcard, not a diary. Don't paste:

- Passwords, IDs, financial info
- Other people's private data
- Anything confidential from a job or internship
- Unpublished work you're not ready to risk

When unsure, leave it out or anonymize it.

## 5. Keep your fingerprints on the work

Use AI to draft, brainstorm, and explain — then **rewrite it in your own voice and make sure you understand every line.** If you couldn't explain it to a friend without the chatbot open, you're not done. The goal is *your* thinking, accelerated — not *its* thinking, borrowed.

## 6. Know your school's rules (before you need them)

Every class will have a different policy on AI — some encourage it, some ban it, most are somewhere in between. **Ask, in writing, before each course's first assignment.** "Is AI allowed for this? For what parts?" Getting that answer early protects you from an academic-integrity mess later. When in doubt, disclose how you used it.

## 7. Build a "trust thermometer"

Calibrate how much to trust an answer by what's at stake:

| Stakes | How much to verify |
|--------|--------------------|
| Brainstorming, explaining a concept to yourself | Low — just sanity-check |
| A draft you'll heavily edit | Medium — verify key facts |
| Anything you'll submit, cite, or publish | High — verify *everything*, cite real sources |
| Health, legal, financial, safety decisions | Don't rely on it — go to a qualified human |

## The mindset, in one line

> **You're the pilot. AI is the autopilot. Autopilot is amazing — and you never take your hands fully off the controls.**
