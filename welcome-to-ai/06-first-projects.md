# Your First Five Projects

Reading about AI is like reading about swimming. Get in the water. Each of these takes 15–45 minutes, needs no coding, and teaches you something real. Pick a free AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini all have free tiers) and go.

> Tip: keep a little doc called `ai-notebook.md` where you paste prompts that worked, answers that surprised you, and times the AI got it wrong. That notebook will be more useful by spring than any tutorial.

---

## Project 1 — The "Explain It Five Ways" warm-up
**Goal: feel how flexible AI is.**

Pick something you find confusing. Ask the AI to explain it:
1. "…like I'm 10."
2. "…like I'm a grad student."
3. "…using an analogy about cooking."
4. "…in exactly 3 bullet points."
5. "…as a short story."

Notice how the *same idea* reshapes itself. That flexibility is the core skill you're learning to direct.

---

## Project 2 — Catch a hallucination on purpose
**Goal: never blindly trust it again.**

Ask: *"Give me five scholarly sources about the history of library classification systems, with authors, titles, and years."*

Now **check every single one** in Google Scholar or your library catalog. Some will be real. Some may be invented — real-sounding author, fake paper. Write down which were fake.

This is the most important lesson in the whole package, and you only really believe it once you've caught it yourself.

---

## Project 3 — Build a research plan from scratch
**Goal: AI as a thinking partner, the honest way.**

Pick a topic you're curious about. Then run this conversation:
1. "Help me turn this broad topic into three specific, debatable research questions."
2. "For my favorite one, what kinds of sources would I need, and where would a librarian look?"
3. "What's the strongest argument *against* my position?"
4. "What am I not thinking of?"

You did the thinking; it widened your view. That's the whole move.

---

## Project 4 — Compare three models on one question
**Goal: learn that "the AI" isn't one thing.**

Ask Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini the *same* meaty question — something like *"What are the biggest ethical risks of using AI in public libraries?"* Put the answers side by side. Where do they agree? Where do they differ? Which felt most honest about uncertainty? You'll quickly develop taste for which tool to reach for when.

---

## Project 5 — Make AI teach you, then quiz you
**Goal: AI as a tutor that adapts to you.**

Pick something from an actual class. Try:
1. "Teach me [topic] step by step, checking my understanding after each step."
2. "Now quiz me with five questions, one at a time, and wait for my answer before giving the next."
3. "I got that wrong — explain why, differently this time."

This is one of the genuinely transformative uses of AI: a patient tutor available at 2 a.m. that never sighs at your questions. Use it a lot.

---

## After these, a few "real life" uses to try

- Paste a dense reading and ask for a plain-English summary — **then read the original** to check it.
- Draft an intimidating email (to a professor, an advisor) and refine the tone together.
- Brainstorm 20 paper topics, throw out 18, keep the 2 that excite you.
- Turn messy notes into an organized outline.

## The rule under all of it

> Every time you use AI, ask: *did this make me understand more, or did it let me understand less?* Aim for "more," every time. That's how you grow instead of shrink.
